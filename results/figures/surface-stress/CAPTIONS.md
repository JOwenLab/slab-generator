# Figure captions - surface stress

Generated by `plot_surface_stress.py`. Every number is derived from
the source files and run directories rather than transcribed.

Sign convention (CLAUDE.md section 2): positive sigma means the cell
is COMPRESSED. tau = sigma*Lz/2 inherits that sign, so positive tau is
a COMPRESSIVE surface stress -- the surface pushes outward and a
released cell expands. This is minus the continuum surface stress f,
for which positive means tensile; consumers wanting f must negate.
The direction is measured, not assumed: free 2D vc-relax expands every
axis with positive tau and contracts the one with negative tau.

All slabs are symmetric H/H terminated at 90/720 Ry, a0 from the
production Birch-Murnaghan bulk fit.

## Figure N1 - Surface stress per facet

Extrapolated surface stress of each H-terminated facet. Each bar runs from tau_xx to tau_yy for one surface, with tau_mean as the open diamond between them and error bars giving the rms of the thickness extrapolation (at most 0.029 N/m, smaller than the markers). Positive tau is a compressive surface stress: the surface pushes outward, so a slab released from the bulk lattice constant expands along that axis, as Figure N4 shows directly. This is minus the continuum surface stress f, for which positive means tensile. The facets span -5.02 to +4.40 N/m and, more importantly, differ in kind. (100) straddles zero, its two in-plane axes carrying surface stress of opposite sign -- compressive along one, tensile along the other, so released it expands on one axis and contracts on the other -- which is the lattice-space origin of a transverse splitting. (111) collapses to a single point: its three-fold symmetry forces tau_xx = tau_yy exactly, making it the natural control surface. Sign of tau_xx - tau_yy depends on the in-plane axis assignment, which is recorded per surface in tau_infinity.csv. Values are read from that file, fitted by fit_tau_infinity.py, and are L2 (thickness-converged, 90/720 Ry).

## Figure N2 - Surface energy versus anisotropy

Surface energy against the magnitude of the surface-stress anisotropy, one point per facet. gamma is read from surface_energy.csv, computed as [E_slab - N_C*mu_C - N_H*mu_H] / 2A at the H-rich limit where mu_H is half the energy of a relaxed H2 molecule at the same 90/720 Ry setting; error bars are the scatter of gamma across the fitted thickness ladder. Negative gamma means the H-terminated face is more stable than bulk diamond plus H2, which is expected because hydrogenation is exothermic. The two quantities are anticorrelated: (111) is the most stable facet and carries exactly zero anisotropy, while (100) carries all of it and is not measurably stabilised by H termination at all (+0.050 +/- 0.024 J/m^2, i.e. indistinguishable from zero within its own ladder scatter). Engineering a transverse NV splitting therefore means favouring the facet that equilibrium morphology suppresses, i.e. a non-equilibrium particle shape. Epistemic level differs by surface ((111) L2; (110) L2; (100) L1): the (100) ladder does not yet support a converged gamma, so its position on this axis is provisional while its anisotropy is not. gamma shifts with the H chemical potential at up to 3.5 J/m^2 per eV, so these are H-rich values and not the whole phase diagram. The shaded direction marks lower gamma, not a Wulff region: a Wulff construction is undefined when gamma is negative.

## Figure N3 - Energy convergence is not stress convergence

Why converging the total energy does not converge the stress. Both quantities are plotted as |Delta| relative to their own value at 120 Ry, each divided by its own converged magnitude, so the two are dimensionless and directly comparable on one log axis; the y value is the fraction of itself that each quantity is still moving by. At 60 Ry the energy per atom is already converged to 2e-05 while the mean in-plane stress is only at 4e-01, and at 100 Ry they stand at 1e-06 and 7e-03. At every cutoff sampled the stress is between 935 and 24227 times less converged than the energy, because the energy is variational in the basis-set error while the stress, its first derivative, is not. A cutoff chosen on an energy criterion alone is therefore not a converged setting for a stress campaign -- the lesson that set 90 Ry here, and the one that recurred on every other axis of this study.

## Figure N4 - vc-relax lattice distortion

In-plane lattice distortion of freely relaxed (vc-relax) slabs against inverse bulk-equivalent thickness, solid for the a axis and dashed for b. Each curve is linear in 1/t and extrapolates to within 0.12% strain at infinite thickness, with fit residuals below 0.012%: the distortion is a surface effect that vanishes in the bulk limit, as it must, which is what validates treating these slabs as models of a surface rather than of a thin film. The 6L rung (struck through) is excluded from the fits, as it is from the thickness ladder: it lies off the 1/t trend, and keeping it would push the largest intercept to 0.64% and the largest residual to 0.150%. The facets differ in sign structure, and that is the physical content: on (100) the two in-plane axes move in opposite directions -- a expands while b contracts -- which is a shear, the lattice-space signature of the transverse-E channel. (111) is exactly isotropic, its two axes equal to numerical precision, so it can only shift D and never split E. (110) expands on both axes but by unequal amounts, so it carries an anisotropy without a sign change. These relaxations also fix the sign of tau independently of any convention: every axis carrying positive tau in Figure N1 expands here and the one carrying negative tau contracts, so positive tau is a compressive surface stress pushing the lattice outward. Strains are relative to each slab's own starting cell at the production a0; positive is expansion. Lines are least-squares fits in 1/t and the only extrapolation in this figure.
